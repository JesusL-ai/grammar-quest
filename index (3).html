Zero Conditional

Facts, rules, routines, and things that are generally true.
If + present simple, present simple
If students practise, they improve.
This describes a general result, not just one future event.
If you heat ice, it melts.
A scientific fact.

Remember: Both clauses usually use the present simple.

🔭

First Conditional

Realistic possibilities and their possible future results.
If + present simple, will + base verb
If I study tonight, I will feel prepared tomorrow.
Studying is a possible action; feeling prepared is a possible result.
If it rains, we will stay inside.
A possible future situation and plan.

Remember: Usually, do not put will directly after if.

Help centre

Questions? Start here.

🧪 When do I use the Zero Conditional?

Use it for general truths, scientific facts, rules, and routines. Example: “If students arrive late, they check in at reception.”

🔭 When do I use the First Conditional?

Use it for a realistic future possibility and its possible result. Example: “If I revise tonight, I will do better tomorrow.”

🧩 Where does “will” go?

In the First Conditional, use present simple after “if” and “will + base verb” in the result clause: “If she studies, she will improve.”

💬 Can I put the “if” clause second?

Yes. “If you practise, you improve” and “You improve if you practise” are both correct. Usually, no comma is needed when the if-clause comes second.

More questions

Can I use “when” instead of “if”?

Sometimes. “When” often suggests that something is expected or certain; “if” presents a condition. For this activity, focus on the target patterns with “if.”

Can I use “may” or “might” instead of “will”?

Yes, other modal verbs can express possibility, but this game focuses on the common First Conditional pattern “if + present simple, will + base verb.”
